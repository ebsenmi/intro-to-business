Dispatch No. 1 · LO 1 — Global Trade

A Small Jar Company Dreams of Going Global

Prairie Jar Co. makes artisan hot sauce and specialty preserves from a facility in Moorhead, Minnesota. After five years of regional success, founder Dana Larsen wants to take the brand international.
By: You — Business Correspondent · Interactive Case Study

Prairie Jar Co. has been one of the Upper Midwest's best-kept culinary secrets since Dana Larsen started selling at the Fargo farmers market in 2019. The company now ships to all 50 states and turns a comfortable profit. But Dana has bigger ambitions.

"I keep getting emails from people in Canada and the UK asking where they can buy our stuff," Dana says, spreading a map across the conference room table. "That's got to mean something."

It does mean something. In fact, 95% of the world's consumers live outside the United States. A business that only sells domestically is ignoring 19 out of every 20 potential customers.

"Are we leaving money on the table — or are we about to make the biggest mistake of our lives?"

Dana's business partner, Marcus, is more cautious. He pulls up a spreadsheet showing how much of their current revenue depends on their domestic supply chain. Going global, he warns, means measuring the risks carefully — including how the value of foreign currencies compared to the dollar will affect what they actually earn.

Dispatch No. 2 · LO 2 — Comparative Advantage

The World Is Open — But Which Door Do You Walk Through?

Prairie Jar Co. is ready to export. But with limited resources, they can only target one international market first. Dana and Marcus analyze their options.
Location: Prairie Jar Co. HQ, Moorhead MN · Day 14 of the Global Push

The team has done their homework. Their hot sauce is fermented using a process that takes three weeks — a production method that requires skilled labor, precise temperature control, and specific pepper varieties grown in the U.S. Midwest. No other country currently makes a product quite like it.

This is their absolute advantage. The product is genuinely unique. Now they need to figure out where to sell it.

Marcus lays out three markets, each with a different profile. "We can't be everywhere at once," he says. "So let's think comparatively about where we actually have the best shot."

Dispatch No. 3 · LO 3 — Barriers to Trade

The Road to Canada Is Paved With Red Tape

Prairie Jar's first export shipment hits a wall — not a physical one, but a bureaucratic one. Not all barriers to trade are created equal.
Location: Canadian Border Crossing · Day 31

The first truck of Prairie Jar product is sitting at the U.S.-Canada border. The driver calls Dana with bad news: their fermented pepper products require a Canadian food certification they don't yet have. The shipment is delayed.

"This isn't a tariff," Dana's trade consultant explains over the phone. "It's a regulatory barrier — a non-tariff barrier. Totally legal, but it still costs you time and money."

While sorting out the certification, Dana gets another call. A U.S. competitor filed a complaint that a European company has been selling a similar fermented sauce in the U.S. at prices far below their actual production cost — apparently to drive domestic producers out of business.

Dispatch No. 4 · LO 4 — Fostering Trade

Who Referees the Global Marketplace?

Prairie Jar's trade consultant explains the three global institutions that make international trade possible — and what each one does when things go wrong.
Location: Video Call · Dana's Home Office · Day 45

"You're going to hear a lot of acronyms in this business," the consultant tells Dana. "Let me break down the three you actually need to know."

First: the WTO — the World Trade Organization. It's the most powerful trade institution in the world. Created by something called the Uruguay Round, which cut global tariffs by one-third overnight. When countries have a trade dispute, the WTO is the referee. You've probably heard about Boeing versus Airbus — the WTO ruled in the U.S.'s favor and allowed $7 billion in retaliatory tariffs against EU subsidies.

Second: the World Bank. It doesn't settle disputes — it makes long-term, low-interest loans to developing nations so they can build ports, roads, and power grids. Better infrastructure means more trade for everyone.

Third: the IMF — the International Monetary Fund. Think of it as the global emergency room for currencies. When a country's balance of payments craters and its currency collapses, the IMF steps in with short-term stabilization loans.

Dispatch No. 5 · LO 5 — Economic Communities

The Club That Changes Everything

Prairie Jar's Canadian shipments are finally flowing. Now Dana learns that the trade agreement governing this relationship almost didn't survive — and what that would have meant.
Location: Prairie Jar Co. Warehouse · Day 60 · First Successful Export

The certification came through. Prairie Jar's first international pallet — 240 cases of Smoky Jalapeño Preserve — crossed into Manitoba without a tariff. Dana grins watching the truck pull out.

"You have USMCA to thank for that," the consultant says. "Used to be called NAFTA. It created a free-trade zone between the U.S., Canada, and Mexico. No tariffs on most goods crossing those borders. The auto industry built its entire supply chain around it — parts cross the border six or seven times before a car is finished."

Dana asks what would happen if USMCA collapsed. "You'd be paying Canadian tariffs on every jar you ship. And Canada could retaliate against U.S. agricultural goods broadly. Same reason Brexit was such a big deal for UK exporters — losing EU membership meant losing zero-tariff access to 26 other countries overnight."

Dispatch No. 6 · LO 6 — Entering the Global Marketplace

The UK Is Calling. How Do You Answer?

A British specialty food importer wants to carry Prairie Jar products. But they're offering something more — a licensing deal that would let them produce a UK version of the sauce locally.
Location: Prairie Jar HQ · Incoming Email from London · Day 78

The email from Hargreaves Fine Foods in London is flattering. They've been following Prairie Jar on social media and think the brand would sell well in UK specialty grocery stores. They're proposing two options.

Option 1: Prairie Jar ships product directly from Moorhead. Hargreaves distributes it in the UK. This is straightforward exporting — Prairie Jar takes currency risk but keeps full control of the product.

Option 2: Prairie Jar licenses its recipes and brand to Hargreaves, who produces a "UK Edition" locally using British peppers. Prairie Jar receives a royalty on every jar sold but gives up some control over the product and quality.

Dana calls Marcus. "Option 2 terrifies me a little," Dana says. "What if they mess up our recipe? But it also means no shipping costs, no ocean freight headaches, no currency exchange on every transaction."

Dispatch No. 7 · LO 7 — Global Threats & Opportunities

Lost in Translation: A Costly Cultural Lesson

Prairie Jar's UK launch stumbles — not because of tariffs or shipping, but because of something the team never thought to check.
Location: London · Post-Launch Review · Day 120

The UK launch should have been a triumph. Instead, it's a headache.

Prairie Jar's marketing team, working from Minnesota, designed the UK campaign using the same messaging they use in the U.S. — heavy on imagery of wide open Midwestern prairies, barns, and American flags. The product tagline: "The Heartland's Hottest."

British food bloggers mocked it. "Why would I want Midwestern American nationalism in my kitchen?" one wrote. Sales in the first month were a fraction of projections.

"You made the classic mistake," a UK marketing consultant told Dana bluntly. "Shared language does not mean shared culture. You assumed Britain was just America with better accents."

Dana thinks of MTV — which had cracked this exact problem by adapting its format entirely for each country rather than exporting American content wholesale. That approach built genuine loyalty. Prairie Jar had done the opposite.

Dispatch No. 8 · LO 8 & 9 — MNCs & Global Trends

Five Years Later: Prairie Jar Goes Global — What Comes Next?

Prairie Jar is now exporting to four countries. A major food conglomerate — a multinational corporation — has made an acquisition offer. Dana faces the biggest decision yet.
Location: Prairie Jar Co. · Five Years Into the Global Journey · Final Dispatch

Prairie Jar Co. is now a genuine international brand. Canada and the UK are steady markets. A distributor in Australia came on board last year. An importer in South Korea reached out last month.

Then the phone call came. GloFoods International — a multinational corporation headquartered in Chicago with production facilities in eight countries — wants to acquire Prairie Jar. The offer is generous.

"Think about what we can do for you," the GloFoods rep says. "We already have facilities in Canada, the UK, and Australia. We can shift production to whichever facility gives us the best cost structure at any given time. If the Canadian dollar tanks, we produce from Chicago. If a UK trade deal falls through, we produce from Australia. That's the advantage of being a multinational."

Dana sits with the offer. Being absorbed by an MNC would mean losing the small-business identity — but gaining production flexibility, resource access across eight countries, and the scale to compete with anyone in the world.
