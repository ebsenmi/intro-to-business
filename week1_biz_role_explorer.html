The Story

Meet Maya & the Mudroom Co.

Maya had a problem. Every time her family came home, the entryway turned into a chaotic pile of shoes, backpacks, and jackets. She looked online for a solution — a simple, attractive mudroom organizer — and couldn't find anything she liked that was also affordable.

So she built one herself in her garage. Her neighbor saw it and wanted one. Then her neighbor's friend. Within three months, Maya was getting more requests than she could handle alone.

She decided to make it a real business. She quit her part-time job, pulled $4,000 from her savings, and launched Mudroom Co. — selling custom entryway organizers directly to homeowners through Instagram and a simple website.

Maya is the only employee. She designs the products, takes the photos, answers customer messages, ships the orders, tracks her spending, and figures out her taxes. She is doing everything — and she is exhausted.

💡 The problem: Maya is great at building things, but she cannot do every business function forever. She needs a team — and each person will have a specific role. That is where you come in.

Step 3 — Chapter 2: Ethics in Business
Maya faces a decision. There is no easy answer.
The Dilemma

Mudroom Co. is growing. A large national retailer reaches out — they want to carry Maya's organizers in 200 stores nationwide. It sounds like a dream opportunity. But to meet their price requirements, Maya would need to make three changes:

❶ Switch to cheaper particle board instead of her current solid wood
❷ Manufacture overseas, cutting production cost by 55%
❸ End her contract with her local supplier — a husband-and-wife business that employs 4 people and depends on Mudroom Co. for 60% of their income

The organizers would look nearly identical to most customers. But Maya knows her current product lasts 8+ years. The new version would likely last 2–3. She has also publicly promised "built to last" quality on her website and social media.

Reasons to take the deal
  • $180,000 in projected new revenue in year one
  • National exposure in 200 retail locations
  • Ability to finally hire staff and scale
  • Less personal financial pressure for Maya
Reasons to walk away
  • Local supplier family loses 60% of their income
  • Product quality drops significantly
  • Her "built to last" promise becomes untrue
  • Risk of reputation damage if quality issues emerge
Before you answer — think about your own life for a moment. Have you ever had to choose between something that was better for you and something that felt more fair or loyal to someone else? A job, a team, a friend group, a school decision? That same tension — opportunity vs. loyalty — is exactly what Maya is feeling right now. Keep that in mind as you write.

What actually tends to happen

Real-world outcomes — neither path is clean

Ethical decisions in business rarely have a clear winner. Here is what research and real business history suggest tends to happen with each path:

If Maya takes the deal

Revenue grows quickly. But within 18 months, customer reviews begin mentioning the product "isn't as solid as it used to be." A popular home design blogger notices and calls it out publicly. Instagram comments turn negative. The retailer threatens to drop the line. Maya now has a brand reputation problem that money cannot easily fix — and a supplier relationship she cannot rebuild.

If Maya turns it down

She keeps her reputation and her supplier relationship. Her loyal customer base grows slowly. But two years later, a competitor launches a nearly identical product at half the price using cheaper materials — and lands in those same 200 stores. Maya is now competing against a version of herself that compromised. Her market shrinks even though she did the right thing.

The point isn't which path is better. It's that both paths have real consequences — for real people, for brand reputation, for long-term survival. That is what ethical business decision-making actually looks like. Easy calls are not ethics. The hard ones are.
