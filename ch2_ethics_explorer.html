The Opening Story

When a Company Named "Honest" Wasn't

In 2015, a company called The Honest Company — co-founded by actress Jessica Alba — was accused of a serious problem. Customers claimed its laundry detergent contained sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), a harsh chemical the company had publicly said its products were free of.

Honest's first move was to call the reporting "reckless" and deny everything. Later, with no public announcement, the company quietly reformulated the detergent and changed the label. No admission. No apology. Just silence and a different formula.

Then more trouble came: baby powder potentially linked to infections, discolored diaper wipes, more product recalls. The company's planned IPO fell through. Acquisition talks with Unilever and Clorox both collapsed. A brand built on the promise of honesty had damaged its reputation by being anything but.

💡 What this activity covers: Chapter 2 gives you the frameworks to understand why this happened — and what businesses can do to make better decisions. You will work through ethical philosophies, decision-making tools, corporate social responsibility, and stakeholder obligations.

Which ethical framework fits each situation?
Four frameworks from Chapter 2 — review before answering
  • Utilitarianism — Choose the action that creates the greatest good for the greatest number of people.
  • Justice / Fairness — Choose the action that distributes burdens and rewards fairly according to accepted societal standards.
  • Deontology — Follow your obligations and duties regardless of the outcome. Do what you are obligated to do.
  • Individual Rights — Protect the fundamental rights every person is born with: life, freedom, and the pursuit of happiness.

Step 3 — Making the Right Call
How do organizations encourage ethical behavior?
Three tools — and two self-tests
  • Leading by example — Managers set the ethical tone. Employees follow what they see, not just what they are told.
  • Ethics training programs — Formal training helps employees recognize questionable situations and practice appropriate responses.
  • Code of ethics — A written document telling employees exactly what the company expects in terms of behavior toward coworkers, customers, and suppliers.

When facing a tough individual decision, two self-tests help:

  • The Feeling Test — How does this action make you feel? If something feels off, it probably is.
  • The Newspaper / Social Media Test — How would an objective reporter describe this decision in a front-page story? Would you be proud of that headline?

Step 4 — CSR Spectrum
Where does each company behavior fall?
Three zones of corporate social responsibility

CSR is the concern of businesses for the welfare of society — going beyond profit and legal requirements. Chapter 2 identifies three categories:

  • Illegal & Irresponsible — Breaks the law and harms society.
  • Legal but Irresponsible — Follows the law, but acts in ways most people consider harmful or unfair.
  • Legal & Responsible — Acts lawfully and actively contributes to stakeholder and societal welfare.

Step 5 — Stakeholder Responsibility
Who does a business owe something to?
Four stakeholder groups from Chapter 2
  • Employees — Safe working conditions, fair pay, empowerment, job security.
  • Customers — Honest products and services, warranties honored, no deceptive claims.
  • Society / the public — Environmental protection, corporate philanthropy, community investment.
  • Investors — Reasonable return on investment and ethical, financially sound management.

One growing trend: strategic philanthropy — aligning charitable giving with the company's own mission and the communities where it operates, rather than simply donating to whoever asks.

Think about a company you interact with regularly — a store, restaurant, streaming service, or employer. You do not need to name it. What you have observed about how it treats people is what matters here.

The bigger picture

Ethics and social responsibility show up in every business decision — from how a company responds to a bad product review, to whether it honors a supplier contract, to who it hires and how it pays them. The Honest Company's story is a reminder that reputation, once damaged, is expensive to rebuild. The frameworks you practiced today are the same tools real managers use when the stakes are real.
