Jordan Alvarez
Taco Terreno · Food Truck · Fargo, ND
24 years old. Launching a food truck selling locally sourced tacos in Fargo. Has $8,000 in personal savings. Wants to operate alone — but the risks are real: hot equipment, food prep, a running vehicle, and hungry customers in close quarters.
Decision 1 of 3
Message from Jordan
"Hey — I'm finally ready to launch. I've got $8K saved, I'm going solo, no partners. My cousin said just file a DBA (Doing Business As) and start selling. But I looked up some food truck horror stories — a truck in Minneapolis got sued when someone said their taco made them sick. Should I just go sole proprietor to keep it simple, or does a food truck actually need an LLC from the start?"
📋 Context
Jordan plans to operate solo with $8,000 in personal savings. A food truck carries layered liability exposure: a moving vehicle that can cause accidents, open flame and hot equipment that can burn customers, and food prepared on-site that can cause illness. Any of these could generate a lawsuit that exceeds the business's cash on hand.
What's your advice, Advisor?
"File a DBA (Doing Business As) and launch as a sole proprietor — it's faster and cheaper. You can always convert to an LLC after you're profitable."
"Incorporate as a C corporation — it offers the strongest liability shield and looks more professional to vendors and customers."
"Set up an LLC before you open. A food truck carries real liability risks — vehicle accidents, burns, food poisoning — and an LLC separates your personal assets from any lawsuit the business faces."
Decision 2 of 3
🚨 Urgent message from Jordan
"I didn't take your advice. I launched as a sole prop to save money on filing fees. I put the whole $8K into the truck and supplies to get started. Now a customer is claiming our salsa gave her food poisoning and she's suing for $55,000. The business only has about $3,200 in it right now. I also have a car and maybe $2,500 in personal savings left. Can she come after my personal stuff?"
📋 Context
Jordan launched as a sole proprietor, investing the full $8,000 into the truck, equipment, and supplies. The business is six months old and the checking account has about $3,200 in it from recent sales. Jordan also personally owns a vehicle and has roughly $2,500 in personal savings. A customer is now suing for $55,000 over alleged food poisoning. As a sole proprietor, there is no legal separation between Jordan and the business.
What's the honest answer?
"As a sole proprietor, there is no legal wall between you and the business. The car, the laptop, the savings — all of it is potentially on the line for the full $55,000."
"You're only liable up to what's in the business account. A registered business name protects your personal assets from lawsuits."
"Food poisoning claims are usually capped at medical expenses only, so the $55,000 demand will likely be reduced significantly in court."
Decision 3 of 3
Message from Jordan
"Good news — Taco Terreno is doing really well. I've saved up enough to buy a second truck and I want to bring in my friend Marcus to run it. Marcus would invest $6,000 and manage that truck full-time while I manage the original one. We'd split profits 60/40 since I'm putting in more. What happens to my LLC when I bring Marcus in — do I need to change my business structure?"
📋 Context
Jordan currently operates Taco Terreno as a single-member LLC. Marcus would invest $6,000 and actively manage the second truck full-time. Jordan wants a 60/40 profit split reflecting the difference in investment. Adding an active co-owner who shares profits and management changes the structure of the LLC.
What's the right advice?
"You should convert to a general partnership — it's simpler than updating an LLC and works fine for two people splitting a business."
"Adding Marcus as an active co-owner turns your single-member LLC into a multi-member LLC. You'll want a written operating agreement that spells out the 60/40 split, each person's role, and what happens if one of you wants to exit."
"Nothing changes — Marcus can just be an employee who happens to share profits. You don't need to update your business structure at all."
✅
Jordan's File Closed
Taco Terreno is headed in the right direction.

Sam Okafor & Riley Chen
CoreFit Studio · Fitness & Training · Moorhead, MN
Best friends turned business partners. Sam runs training programs and client relationships; Riley manages finances and marketing. Equal financial investment. Currently operating on a verbal agreement only.
Decision 1 of 4
Message from Sam & Riley
"We're finally opening CoreFit next month! We split the startup costs 50/50, Sam runs the floor and I handle the books — we each put in the same amount and we're both working full time in the business. What type of partnership are we? Someone at the bank asked and we didn't know what to say."
📋 Context
Both Sam and Riley invested equally, actively manage the business, share profits, and are both personally involved in daily operations. Neither is a silent or passive investor.
What's your advice, Advisor?
"You're in a limited partnership — Riley is the limited partner since Riley handles back-office work, not client-facing operations."
"You're in a general partnership — both of you actively manage the business, share profits equally, and each of you can make decisions that legally bind the other."
"You're co-sole proprietors — since you each own 50%, each of you is running your own separate sole proprietorship."
Decision 2 of 4
Urgent message from Sam
"Riley just signed a lease on $18,000 worth of new equipment without telling me. I had no idea — I found out when the invoice showed up. I disagree with this purchase completely. Am I legally on the hook for this even though I didn't sign anything?"
📋 Context
CoreFit is a general partnership. Riley signed a vendor contract alone, without Sam's prior knowledge or approval. The equipment has already been delivered.
How do you explain their legal situation?
"No — since Sam didn't sign the contract, Sam has no legal obligation. Only Riley is liable for this purchase."
"It depends — you're only liable if the purchase was over a threshold amount set in your partnership agreement."
"Yes — in a general partnership, any partner can legally bind the business. Both of you are on the hook for this contract, even though Sam didn't sign it."
Decision 3 of 4
Message from Riley
"A client is threatening to sue us for $90,000 — says she was injured in one of Sam's classes. CoreFit only has $20,000 in the business account. Can she come after our personal bank accounts and property if the business can't cover it?"
📋 Context
CoreFit is currently a general partnership. The lawsuit amount is $90,000. Business account has $20,000. Both Sam and Riley own personal vehicles and have personal savings accounts.
What do you tell them?
"Yes — as general partners, both of you face unlimited personal liability. The full $90,000 claim can reach your personal savings, cars, and other assets."
"The liability is capped at what's in the business account — $20,000. Your personal assets are protected by the fact that you're a registered business."
"Since the injury happened in Sam's class, only Sam is personally liable — not Riley."
Decision 4 of 4
Message from Sam & Riley
"Our attorney is pushing us to convert to an LLC and also write a formal partnership agreement. We already have a verbal understanding — is a written agreement really necessary? And will an LLC mean we lose control of our own gym?"
📋 Context
Sam and Riley currently operate on a verbal agreement with no written terms. Their attorney is recommending both an LLC conversion for liability protection and a written operating agreement to define roles, profit splits, and exit procedures.
What's your recommendation?
"The verbal agreement is fine — courts generally honor informal business arrangements between established partners."
"Yes to both. An LLC protects your personal assets without removing your management control — you can run it exactly as you do now. And the written agreement is critical: it defines what happens if one of you wants to exit, disagrees, or can no longer participate."
"Convert to an LLC, but skip the written agreement — you know each other well enough that the formality will create unnecessary tension."
✅
CoreFit's File Closed
Sam and Riley have a real plan now.

Nova Tech — Maya Patel, CEO
Software Company · Fargo, ND · 47 Employees
Started in a Fargo basement five years ago. Now projecting $4.2M in revenue. Recently incorporated as a C corporation. Evaluating major structural decisions: franchise expansion, a potential acquisition, and an unsolicited merger offer.
Decision 1 of 3
Message from Maya Patel, CEO
"We just incorporated as a C corp and our CFO mentioned something called 'double taxation.' Our board wants me to understand this before our next investor meeting. Can you explain exactly what double taxation means for us — and whether it's actually a problem?"
📋 Context
Nova Tech is now a C corporation with outside investors who will receive dividends. The board wants to ensure Maya understands the tax structure before discussing profit distributions.
How do you explain it?
"Double taxation means Nova Tech pays corporate income tax on its profits — then when you distribute those profits to shareholders as dividends, they pay personal income tax on them again. The same money gets taxed twice."
"Double taxation means you pay taxes in both the state you're incorporated in and every state you operate in — but most corporations get around this with interstate tax agreements."
"It means the IRS charges you twice for the same transaction — once when you invoice a client and once when you collect payment."
Decision 2 of 3
Message from Maya Patel, CEO
"We just purchased DataBridge — a smaller analytics startup. We absorbed their whole team and their technology stack into Nova Tech. DataBridge no longer exists as a company. Meanwhile, a national firm called TechHorizon wants to merge with us — they're proposing we become a new company called 'Nova Horizon' together. What's the difference between what we did with DataBridge and what TechHorizon is proposing?"
📋 Context
Nova Tech acquired DataBridge (DataBridge ceased to exist). TechHorizon is now proposing a full merger where both companies would dissolve and form a new combined entity with shared leadership.
How do you explain the difference?
"They're basically the same thing — 'merger' and 'acquisition' are interchangeable terms for when companies combine."
"A merger means you buy TechHorizon. An acquisition means they buy you. The difference is just about who initiates the deal."
"With DataBridge, you did an acquisition — you purchased and absorbed them, and they ceased to exist. TechHorizon is proposing a merger — both companies would dissolve and become a brand new, combined entity together. In a merger, neither original company survives independently."
Decision 3 of 3
Message from Maya Patel, CEO
"We're thinking about selling franchises of our cloud software platform to regional IT firms. They'd operate under the Nova Tech name and use our systems, and we'd collect a licensing fee. My team keeps calling us 'the franchisee' in meetings. Is that right — and what exactly would our role be in this arrangement?"
📋 Context
Nova Tech would be licensing its brand, systems, processes, and support to independent regional operators in exchange for fees. Nova Tech owns the brand and would set the operating standards.
What do you tell Maya?
"Your team is right — Nova Tech would be the franchisee since you'd be sharing your systems with others."
"Actually, Nova Tech would be the franchisor — you own the brand and system. The regional IT firms who pay to operate under your name are the franchisees. You set the rules; they run the locations."
"Nova Tech would be a silent partner in each regional operation — you provide the brand but take no active role."
✅
Nova Tech's File Closed
Maya has the clarity she needs to move forward.
