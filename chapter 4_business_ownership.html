📷 Scenario 1 — Sneaker Resale

Meet Jordan — The Sneaker Guy

Jordan is a 20-year-old college junior who started flipping limited-release sneakers out of his dorm room. Last semester he made $34,000 in sales and pocketed $8,000 in profit. His roommate Marcus wants to invest $5,000 to help buy more inventory. A local sneaker boutique just offered him shelf space on consignment.

Jordan has been operating with no formal business structure. His accounting professor told him: “You need structure.” Jordan needs something that protects him personally, allows Marcus to officially join, and sets the business up to grow.

💵 $34K annual sales🤝 Partner wants in ($5K)🏭 Retail deal on the table⚠️ Liability risk if sued

♨ Scenario 3 — Farming Community

Meet the Millbrook Growers

Eight small-scale vegetable farmers in rural Minnesota have been selling independently at local farmers markets for years. Individually, none of them can afford refrigerated trucks, a commercial kitchen, or the marketing needed to reach grocery stores. They are each profitable at small scale but stuck there.

A community development advisor suggests they consider joining forces as a group rather than competing. They want a structure where every farmer has an equal voice, profits are shared based on what each member contributes, and no single person owns or controls the whole operation. Their goal is collective strength without surrendering individual identity.

🌿 8 independent farmers⚖️ Equal voice for each member🚚 Shared trucks and kitchen📈 Collective market access

🤝 Scenario 4 — Landscaping Startup

Meet Carlos & DeShawn — The Lawn Guys

Carlos and DeShawn are best friends who both got laid off from the same landscaping company on the same day. They have decades of combined experience, a shared trailer full of equipment, and a list of 30 former clients willing to follow them. They want to start their own landscaping business immediately.

They have very little money to spend on legal or filing fees and need to start taking jobs this week. They trust each other completely, plan to split all revenue and decisions equally, and are not worried about liability right now — their clients are all residential homeowners and the work is low-risk. Speed and simplicity matter more than anything else.

⏱️ Need to start immediately💵 Almost no startup capital🤝 Equal trust and split🏠 Low liability risk (residential)

🧑 Scenario 5 — Farmers Market Baker

Meet Priya — The Weekend Baker

Priya bakes specialty South Asian desserts every weekend and sells them at two local farmers markets. She works completely alone, makes everything in her certified home kitchen, and averages $800–$1,200 per weekend in sales. She has no employees, no partners, no investors, and no plans to expand beyond her current markets.

Priya’s accountant friend mentioned she should formalize her business for tax purposes — mainly so she can deduct her ingredient costs, equipment, and market booth fees. She wants the simplest possible structure with minimal paperwork, minimal cost, and zero complexity. She is happy staying small.

🧑 Solo operation🏠 Home kitchen, no employees💵 $800–$1,200/weekend✅ Wants minimal complexity

🏢 Scenario 6 — Tech Startup

Meet Simone — The Founder

Simone has spent three years building a software platform that helps small medical practices manage patient scheduling and billing. She has paying customers, a working product, and a co-founder. Now she is ready to raise serious outside investment — she is pitching venture capital firms next month and needs a structure that lets her issue equity to investors and offer stock options to the engineers she wants to hire.

Her startup attorney is unambiguous: “You need to incorporate before you walk into that pitch meeting.” Simone needs the strongest possible liability protection, the ability to issue different classes of stock, and a structure that signals to investors she is serious and ready to scale.

💰 Raising venture capital📊 Issuing equity to investors👨‍💻 Hiring with stock options⚖️ Signals credibility to investors
